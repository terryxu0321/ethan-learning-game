## A. Developmental psychology of interests

- **[S1]** *Planes, trains, automobiles — and tea sets: Extremely intense interests in very young children.* DeLoache, J. S., Simcock, G., & Macari, S. *Developmental Psychology* 43(6), 1579–1586. 2007. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18020834/ — Peer-reviewed journal article. © APA, all rights reserved; only the citation and short paraphrase are used here. Note: surveys and interviews with 177 parents found that nearly one third of young children have "extremely intense interests". These interests are much more common in boys and can start in toddlerhood. Boys' interests often centre on a single object category, such as vehicles or dinosaurs, while girls' interests lean more towards pretend play.
- **[S2]** *The development of conceptual interests in young children.* Alexander, J. M., Johnson, K. E., Leibham, M. E., & Kelley, K. *Cognitive Development* 23(2), 324–334. 2008. https://www3.nd.edu/~kkelley/publications/articles/Alexander_Johnson_Leibham_Kelley_CD_2008.pdf (author's personal copy) — Peer-reviewed article. © Elsevier; the author's copy is not openly licensed, so it was not saved. Note: a longitudinal study of ages 4–6. Conceptual interests (for example dinosaurs or science topics) are common and more frequent in boys. The likelihood of having one declined after school entry, at a similar rate for boys and girls. Popular coverage of this work (secondary) says children with these interests showed more persistence, attention and knowledge; that link is correlational.
- **[S3]** *Science interests in preschool boys and girls: Relations to later self-concept and science achievement.* Leibham, M. B., Alexander, J. M., & Johnson, K. E. *Science Education* 97(4), 574–593. 2013. https://doi.org/10.1002/sce.21066 — Peer-reviewed article. © Wiley. Note: 116 children were followed from ages 4–6 to age 8. Boys had higher science interest overall. Girls' early intense science interests predicted a higher science self-concept and higher achievement at age 8, which supports nurturing these interests in *all* children.
- **[S4]** *Longitudinal analysis of the relations between opportunities to learn about science and the development of interests related to science.* Alexander, J. M., Johnson, K. E., & Kelley, K. *Science Education* 96(5), 763–786. 2012. https://doi.org/10.1002/sce.21018 — Peer-reviewed article. © Wiley. Note: a study of 192 children aged 4–7 using parent reports. It links children's opportunities to learn about science (books, outings, conversations) to the development of science-related interests.
- **[S5]** *The four-phase model of interest development.* Hidi, S., & Renninger, K. A. *Educational Psychologist* 41(2), 111–127. 2006. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4 (summary at https://www.informalscience.org/four-phase-model-interest-development) — Theoretical review. © Taylor & Francis. Note: interest develops in four phases: triggered situational interest, maintained situational interest, emerging individual interest and well-developed individual interest. The environment and other people can support each phase.
- **[S6]** *Trains, Dolls and Dinosaurs: Why Do Kids Get Obsessed?* Live Science. Date not verified (circa 2016). https://livescience.com/55492-why-kids-have-obsessions.html — Science journalism. © Future plc. Note: popular summary of DeLoache's work, including her view that intense interests are generally nothing to worry about.
- **[S7]** *How large are gender differences in toy preferences? A systematic review and meta-analysis of toy preference research.* Davis, J. T. M., & Hines, M. *Archives of Sexual Behavior* 49, 373–394. 2020. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-019-01624-7 — Meta-analysis. Springer; the license of the version of record was not verified (it may be open access), so nothing was saved. Note: across 75 studies, children on average show large preferences for toys typed to their own gender. Differences grow with age and are larger under forced-choice methods. These are group averages and say nothing certain about any individual child.
- **[S8]** Commentary on gendered toy marketing by Beatrice Alba (Deakin University). *BroadAgenda*, University of Canberra. Date not verified. https://broadagenda.com.au/?p=4304 — Expert commentary/blog. © publisher. Note: summarises the argument by Fine and Rush that gendered marketing narrows what children believe is "for" them. Used here only to support the design principle of offering choice rather than assigning themes by gender.
- **[S9]** *The impact of pretend play on children's development: A review of the evidence.* Lillard, A. S., et al. *Psychological Bulletin* 139(1), 1–34. 2013. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029321 — Systematic review. © APA. Note: the evidence is insufficient to call pretend play *crucial*, but it may contribute to language, narrative, social development and self-regulation. Pretend play is useful as a context for learning, not as a guaranteed driver of it.
- **[S10]** *Children's questions: A mechanism for cognitive development.* Chouinard, M. M. *Monographs of the Society for Research in Child Development* 72(1). 2007. Catalogue record: https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsjsr,edsjsr.30163594 — Research monograph. © SRCD/Wiley. Note: young children ask many information-seeking questions and keep asking when they get no answer. Curiosity is goal-directed.
- **[S11]** *Observing the unexpected enhances infants' learning and exploration.* Stahl, A. E., & Feigenson, L. *Science* 348, 91–94. 2015. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25838378/ — Peer-reviewed experiment. © AAAS; an NIH author manuscript exists on PMC (PMC5861377), but it carries no CC license, so it was not saved. Note: infants learned more about objects that violated their expectations. This is the basis for using "surprise" mechanics, extrapolated from infants.

## B. Popularity and media data

- **[S12]** *The 2025 Common Sense Census: Media Use by Kids Zero to Eight.* Common Sense Media. 2025-02-26. https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/the-2025-common-sense-census-media-use-by-kids-zero-to-eight — Nationally representative US survey report. © Common Sense Media; free to read but not openly licensed. Note (from the official summary and press coverage): screen time is about 2.5 h/day for ages 0–8; gaming is up roughly 65% since 2020; about 58% of children have their own tablet by age 4; daily reading among 5–8-year-olds fell from 64% to 52% since 2017.
- **[S13]** *Children and Parents: Media Use and Attitudes Report 2025.* Ofcom (UK). 2025-05-07. https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/media-habits-children/children-and-parents-media-use-and-attitudes-report-2025 — Government regulator report. Ofcom content is often reusable under its own terms or the OGL, but the license for this specific report was **not verified** and the page was unreachable, so nothing was saved. Note (secondary, via a Twinkl summary): about 94% of 5–7-year-olds use video-sharing sites. Creative games such as Roblox and Minecraft are popular with children.
- **[S14]** Circana Toy Industry Performance Awards. Global and US results for 2024 sales (announced January 2025) and 2025 sales (announced 2026). ## C. Mechanics, personalisation and learning-design evidence

- **[S20]** Personalised-book engagement study (exact title not verified). Kucirkova, N., Messer, D., & Whitelock, D. *Journal of Early Childhood Literacy*. 2013. Accepted manuscript: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1536714/ — Small observational study (n=7 dyads, ages 12–33 months). Green open-access manuscript; reuse license not verified. Note: books featuring the child's name and photos drew more smiles, laughter and vocal activity than comparable books.
- **[S21]** *An empirical investigation of parent-child shared reading of digital personalized books.* Kucirkova, N., Gattis, M., Spargo, T., Seisdedos de Vega, B., & Flewitt, R. *International Journal of Educational Research* 105, 101710. 2021. https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/627018 and https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/136618/ — Peer-reviewed experiment. Reported as **CC BY** in the repository; not saved because the fetch was blocked. Note: compared word learning on personalised and non-personalised pages of digital books. A secondary report says personalisation was associated with better word learning; check the paper for exact effects.
- **[S22]** *Mine, mine, mine: Self-reference and children's retention of novel words.* Axelsson, E. L., et al. *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:958. 2018. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00958/full — Peer-reviewed experiment. Frontiers is **CC BY** (standard license); not saved because the fetch was blocked. Note: 3-year-olds remembered new words better right away when the objects were "theirs". The advantage faded by the next day.
- **[S23]** Calvert, S. L., et al. Intelligent Dora character and young children's math learning. *Child Development*. 2019. Coverage: Georgetown College news, https://college.georgetown.edu/news-story/study-finds-childrens-math-learning-improves-by-interacting-with-intelligent-characters/ ; EdWeek, 2019-12 — Peer-reviewed experiment (via university release). © publishers. Note: 217 children aged 3–6. Children who felt a closer "friendship" with the character answered math problems faster and more accurately. Character feedback and math talk mattered.
- **[S24]** *Animal companions: Fostering children's effort-making by nurturing virtual pets.* Chen, Z.-H., Chan, T.-W., et al. (full author list not verified) *British Journal of Educational Technology*. 2011. https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/animal-companions-fostering-childrens-effort-making-by-nurturing--2/ — Peer-reviewed study (small samples, primary-school age). © Wiley/BERA. Note: a virtual pet that grows with the child's learning effort (My-Pet) improved the quality of effort-making behaviours. This is promising but not proven for 5-year-olds.
- **[S25]** *Undermining children's intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward: A test of the "overjustification" hypothesis.* Lepper, M. R., Greene, D., & Nisbett, R. E. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 28(1), 129–137. 1973. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0035519 — Classic experiment with preschoolers. © APA. Note: preschoolers who already liked drawing and were *promised* a certificate later drew less in free play. Children given surprise rewards or no reward did not.
- **[S26]** *A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation.* Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R. M. *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6), 627–668. 1999. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627 — Meta-analysis. © APA. Note: expected tangible rewards tend to undermine intrinsic motivation, while informational verbal praise tends to support it (effect sizes are quoted only in secondary sources).
- **[S27]** *The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation and related outcomes: A meta-analysis.* Patall, E. A., Cooper, H., & Robinson, J. C. *Psychological Bulletin* 134(2), 270–300. 2008. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18298272/ — Meta-analysis (41 studies). © APA. Note: choice increased intrinsic motivation, effort and performance. Effects were larger for children and with 2–4 choices, including "instructionally irrelevant" ones such as choosing a colour or theme.
- **[S28]** *Advertising in young children's apps: A content analysis.* Meyer, M., Adkins, V., Yuan, N., Weeks, H. M., Chang, Y.-J., & Radesky, J. *Journal of Developmental & Behavioral Pediatrics* 40(1). 2019. https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/706093 — Content analysis. © Wolters Kluwer. Note: 95% of the 135 apps coded for children 5 and under contained advertising, including manipulative designs such as sad characters when a child declines a purchase. These are anti-patterns to avoid.
- **[S29]** *Visual environment, attention allocation, and learning in young children: When too much of a good thing may be bad.* Fisher, A. V., Godwin, K. E., & Seltman, H. *Psychological Science* 25(7). 2014. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614533801 ; CMU news, https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2014/may/may27_decoratedclassrooms.html — Experiment with kindergarteners. © SAGE. Note: children in a heavily decorated classroom were more off-task and learned less. Keep themed decoration away from the task area.
- **[S30]** *When too much of a novel thing may be what's "bad": commentary on Fisher, Godwin, and Seltman (2014).* Imuta, K., & Scarf, D. *Frontiers in Psychology* 5:1444. 2014. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01444/full — Commentary. Frontiers **CC BY** (standard); not saved because the fetch was blocked. Note: argues that the novelty of the decorations may explain the distraction effect, so familiar, consistent visuals may matter less.
- **[S31]** Walkington, C., & Bernacki, M. L. (2018). Study of context personalization in algebra (150 high-school students, 4 units in an intelligent tutor), *Journal of Educational Psychology* (exact title not verified), plus a companion 2018 paper in *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education*. Related AIED paper: https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40593-018-0168-1.pdf ; summary: https://edsurge.com/n/2013-11-08-can-interest-based-personalization-deliver-better-learning-outcomes — Peer-reviewed studies (high-school algebra). Springer AIED article license not verified. Note: personalising problem *contexts* to students' interests raised situational interest and performance. The effect depended on how *deeply* the context connected to the math, not on surface swaps. This evidence comes from older students, so extrapolating it to age 5 is an assumption.
- **[S32]** *A meta-analysis of the cognitive and motivational effects of serious games.* Wouters, P., van Nimwegen, C., van Oostendorp, H., & van der Spek, E. D. *Journal of Educational Psychology* 105(2), 249–265. 2013. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031311 — Meta-analysis. © APA. Note: serious games improved learning (d≈0.29) and retention (d≈0.36) compared with conventional instruction but were *not* significantly more motivating. Learning gains were larger with multiple sessions and when games were combined with other instruction.
- **[S33]** *Digital games, design, and learning: A systematic review and meta-analysis.* Clark, D. B., Tanner-Smith, E. E., & Killingsworth, S. S. *Review of Educational Research* 86(1), 79–122. 2016. Brief: https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/digital-learning-pubs/digital-games-design-and-learning-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-brief/ — Meta-analysis (K-16). © AERA/SAGE. Note: games beat non-game conditions (g≈0.33), and design enhancements added more (g≈0.34). Game mechanics and narrative features moderated the effects, so design matters more than the medium.
- **[S34]** *Putting education in "educational" apps: Lessons from the science of learning.* Hirsh-Pasek, K., Zosh, J. M., Golinkoff, R. M., Gray, J. H., Robb, M. B., & Kaufman, J. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 16(1), 3–34. 2015. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html — Review/framework. © SAGE/APS. Note: proposes four pillars (active/"minds-on", engaged without distraction, meaningful, socially interactive) within a clear learning goal.
- **[S35]** *Belonging, Being and Becoming: The Early Years Learning Framework for Australia V2.0.* Australian Government Department of Education / ACECQA. 2022. https://www.acecqa.gov.au/nqf/national-law-regulations/approved-learning-frameworks — Official curriculum framework. **CC BY 4.0** for the text (photographs and trademarks excluded); not saved because the fetch was blocked. Note: Australian early-childhood programs must be based on each child's "developmental needs, interests and experiences", and the framework emphasises play-based learning. This is the policy context Ethan will meet.
- **[S36]** *Kids don't engage? Connect learning with their interests* (reporting a University of South Australia study in the *International Journal of Early Childhood*). Phys.org / News-Medical. 2023-11-10. https://phys.org/news/2023-11-kids-dont-engage.html — News coverage of a qualitative study. © publishers. Note: when teachers rebuilt the curriculum around the interests and motivations of disengaged preschoolers, those children became more engaged, involved and confident.
- **[S37]** *Designing for Children* (video and articles). Nielsen Norman Group. Date not verified. https://www.nngroup.com/videos/designing-children/ — UX research summary. © NN/g. Note: children aged 3–5 are pre-readers and need clear, specific instructions that use mental models they already have. Advertising and clutter confuse young users.
