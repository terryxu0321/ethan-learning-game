## A. Australian Early Development Census (AEDC) and its predictive evidence

- **[S1]** *About the AEDC domains.* Australian Government / AEDC. Undated web page. https://www.aedc.gov.au/about-the-aedc/about-the-aedc-domains — Official program page. Commonwealth content; site licence not verified (many Australian Government sites use CC BY 4.0, 需核实). Note: lists the five domains. Physical health and wellbeing covers coping with the school day, independence, motor skills and energy. Social competence covers getting along, responsibility and respect, approaches to learning and readiness to explore. Emotional maturity covers aggression, anxiety, impulsivity, concentration and helping others. Language and cognitive skills (school-based) covers basic and advanced literacy, basic numeracy, and interest in literacy and numeracy plus memory. Communication skills and general knowledge covers storytelling, talking with children and adults, and articulation. Sub-domain wording was cross-checked against council and state guides (Burwood 2024 community profile; Qld *AEDC guide*; WA family guide).
- **[S2]** *How to interpret data* / *Understanding the data.* AEDC. Undated. https://www.aedc.gov.au/data-hub/interpreting-data/how-to-interpret-data ; https://www.aedc.gov.au/resources/detail/understanding-the-data — Official methodology page. Licence as [S1]. Note: teachers answer about 100 questions per child, and each domain is scored 0–10. Cut-offs were fixed in 2009. "On track" means above the 25th percentile, "at risk" means between the 10th and 25th percentiles, and "developmentally vulnerable" means below the 10th percentile. The AEDC is a **population** measure and is not designed to assess individual children.
- **[S3]** *AEDC National Report 2024* and *The 2024 AEDC results have been released* (news, 2025-06-13). Australian Government / AEDC. 2025. https://www.aedc.gov.au/news-and-media/details/the-2024-aedc-results-have-been-released ; national report PDF: https://www.aedc.gov.au/docs/default-source/default-document-library/aedc_national-report-2024_da7-409a37b9d-95ce-43e7-af00-51aa604cf0b3.pdf — Official national report. Licence as [S1]. Note: in 2024, 52.9% of children were on track on all five domains, 23.5% were vulnerable on 1 or more domains, and 12.5% on 2 or more (the highest of the six collections). By domain, the share vulnerable was: physical 10.0% (+0.2 points), social 10.7% (+1.1), emotional 10.0% (+1.5, the largest rise), language/cognitive 7.7% (+0.4) and communication/general knowledge 8.9% (+0.5).
- **[S4]** *Associations between the Early Development Instrument at age 5, and reading and numeracy skills at ages 8, 10 and 12: a prospective linked data study.* Brinkman, S. et al. *Child Indicators Research* 6, 695–708. 2013. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12187-013-9189-3 — Peer-reviewed article. © Springer. Note: WA EDI records were linked to NAPLAN for n = 1,823 children. School-entry scores predicted Year 3, 5 and 7 literacy and numeracy, and the association was equally strong at each age. Exact title and page numbers 需核实.
- **[S5]** *Building the best communities for early childhood development.* Telethon Kids Institute (news). 2016-05. https://donate.telethonkids.org.au/news--events/news-and-events-nav/2016/may/building-the-best-communities-for-early-child/ — Research institute news. © Telethon Kids. Note: children vulnerable on the language and cognitive domain, or on the communication and general knowledge domain, had lower Year 3 NAPLAN reading and numeracy and "do not catch up".
- **[S6]** *Literacy and numeracy skills for future learning* (research summary). Australian Education Research Organisation (AERO). 2023-11. https://www.edresearch.edu.au/summaries-explainers/research-summaries/skills-future-literacy-and-numeracy-learning-research-summary ; PDF: https://www.edresearch.edu.au/sites/default/files/2023-11/literacy-numeracy-skills-future-learning-research-summary-aa.pdf — Government research body report. Licence not verified (AERO commonly uses CC BY 4.0, 需核实). Note: links AEDC data to NAPLAN and finds that skills at school entry significantly predict achievement three years later, with independent effects still visible in Year 5. It maps the language and cognitive domain to the Australian Curriculum and the National Literacy and Numeracy Learning Progressions. A secondary summary says this domain had the strongest association with Year 3 achievement; that ranking is 需核实 in the full report.
- **[S7]** *Australia's children — The transition to primary school.* Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW). Undated web report. https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/children-youth/australias-children/contents/education/transition-primary-school — Government statistical report. AIHW usually publishes under CC BY 4.0 (需核实 for this page). Note: describes the five AEDC domains as constructs of readiness. It cites research that children vulnerable on 1 or more domains at age 5 were more likely to score in the bottom 20% of NAPLAN in Years 3, 5 and 7.
- **[S8]** *Research Spotlight: Australian Early Development Census 2024.* Mitchell Institute, Victoria University. 2025-08. https://content.vu.edu.au/sites/default/files/documents/2025-08/mitchell-institute-research-spotlight-aedc-2024.pdf — Policy research brief. © Victoria University. Note: secondary analysis of the 2024 increase in vulnerability. Reports a 94.6% participation rate.

## C. Research evidence on school readiness

- **[S20]** *School readiness and later achievement.* Duncan, G. J., et al. *Developmental Psychology* 43(6), 1428–1446. 2007. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18020822/ (author copy: https://sites.uci.edu/gduncan/files/2021/03/School-readiness-pdf.pdf) — Meta-analysis of 6 longitudinal datasets. © APA. Note: school-entry maths was the strongest predictor of later achievement, followed by reading and then attention. Socioemotional behaviour measures were generally **not** significant predictors of later academic scores. The samples were general-population. Critics such as C. Blair questioned the conclusions (secondary).
- **[S21]** McClelland, M. M., et al. (2007). *Links between behavioral regulation and preschoolers' literacy, vocabulary, and math skills.* *Developmental Psychology* 43(4), 947–959; and Ponitz, C. C., McClelland, M. M., et al. (2009). *A structured observation of behavioral self-regulation and its contribution to kindergarten outcomes.* *Developmental Psychology* 45(3), 605–619. Summary: Oregon State University Kindergarten Readiness Research Program, https://health.oregonstate.edu/research/kreadiness/research — Peer-reviewed articles, accessed via the program summary. © APA. Note: behavioural regulation (the Head-Toes-Knees-Shoulders task) predicted literacy, vocabulary and maths in preschool (about 310 children) and kindergarten (343 children). Strong fall performers were about 3.4 months ahead in maths. The pattern held in samples from the US and East Asia. Most evidence comes from the same lab. Citation details 需核实.
- **[S22]** *A gradient of childhood self-control predicts health, wealth, and public safety.* Moffitt, T. E., et al. *PNAS* 108(7), 2693–2698. 2011. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1010076108 — Peer-reviewed cohort study (Dunedin, n ≈ 1,000, followed to age 32; plus about 500 UK twin pairs). © PNAS. Note: childhood self-control predicted adult health, finances, substance dependence and offending, independent of IQ and social class.
- **[S23]** *Interventions shown to aid executive function development in children 4 to 12 years old.* Diamond, A., & Lee, K. *Science* 333, 959–964. 2011. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21852486/ — Review. © AAAS (an author manuscript is on PMC: PMC3159917; licence not verified). Note: computerised training, non-computer games, aerobics, martial arts, yoga, mindfulness and curricula can all improve executive function. Successful programs use repeated practice with increasing challenge. Children with weaker executive function benefit most. Narrow EF training may be less effective than also addressing emotional, social and physical development.
- **[S24]** *The impact of enhancing students' social and emotional learning: A meta-analysis of school-based universal interventions.* Durlak, J. A., et al. *Child Development* 82(1), 405–432. 2011. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x — Meta-analysis (213 programs, 270,034 K–12 students). © Wiley. Note: SEL programs improved skills, attitudes and behaviour, and an 11-percentile-point achievement gain was found in the smaller subset of studies that measured it. The programs were delivered at school by people (not apps).
- **[S25]** Cameron, C. E., et al. (2012). *Fine motor skills and executive function both contribute to kindergarten achievement.* *Child Development* 83, 1229–1244. Grissmer, D., et al. (2010). *Fine motor skills and early comprehension of the world.* *Developmental Psychology* 46(5), 1008–1017 (需核实). Records: https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/19534cbd-dd11-4b5d-8262-a17df658cdc4/full — Peer-reviewed. © Wiley / APA. Note: fine motor skills (especially visuomotor integration) and EF each independently contribute to kindergarten achievement. Commercial claims that fine motor skills are "the #1 predictor" are not supported.
- **[S26]** Jago, L. S., Monaghan, P., Alcock, K., & Cain, K. (2025). Meta-analysis of preschool vocabulary and grammar predicting early reading. Repository record: https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/228422 — Meta-analysis (72 longitudinal studies, 23,387 children). Licence not verified. Note: preschool vocabulary and grammar each had moderate effects on reading comprehension, word reading and pseudoword reading at the start of formal schooling. Journal name and exact effect sizes 需核实.
- **[S27]** *Early childhood curiosity and kindergarten reading and math academic achievement.* Shah, P. E., Weeks, H. M., Richards, B., & Kaciroti, N. *Pediatric Research* 84, 380–386. 2018. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41390-018-0039-3 (accepted manuscript: https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10119313) — Peer-reviewed cohort analysis (ECLS-B, about 6,200 children). © Springer Nature. Note: parent-rated curiosity was associated with higher kindergarten reading and maths, and the association was stronger in low-SES children. Volume and pages 需核实.
- **[S28]** *Cognitive and academic benefits of music training with children: A multilevel meta-analysis.* Sala, G., & Gobet, F. *Memory & Cognition* 48, 1429–1441. 2020. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7683441 — Meta-analysis (54 studies, 6,984 participants). Reported as open access (CC BY 4.0, 需核实). Note: once study quality is controlled, the effect of music training on cognitive and academic outcomes is about zero. Music improves music skills but does not transfer to maths or reading.
- **[S29]** *Effect of classroom-based physical activity interventions on academic and physical activity outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Watson, A., Timperio, A., Brown, H., Best, K., & Hesketh, K. D. *International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity* 14, 114. 2017. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-017-0569-9 — Systematic review and meta-analysis (39 studies, 16 meta-analysed). **CC BY 4.0** (BioMed Central standard; download blocked, so not saved). Note: reviews classroom movement breaks and on-task behaviour, cognition and achievement in primary children. Pooled effect sizes were not read (需核实).
- **[S30]** *The effects of handwriting experience on functional brain development in pre-literate children.* James, K. H., & Engelhardt, L. *Trends in Neuroscience and Education* 1(1), 32–42. 2012. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4274624 — Small fMRI experiment with 5-year-olds. © Elsevier (NIH manuscript; licence not verified). Note: the brain's "reading circuit" was recruited for letters after **handwriting** practice but not after typing or **tracing**. It is a small single study, so the evidence is suggestive.

## D. Digital media for young children

- **[S31]** *Putting education in "educational" apps: Lessons from the science of learning.* Hirsh-Pasek, K., et al. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 16(1), 3–34. 2015. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/educational-apps.html — Review. © APS/SAGE. Note: four pillars for learning apps: active ("minds-on"), engaged (not distracted), meaningful, and socially interactive, all within a clear learning goal. A later audit of 100 popular apps found low scores on all pillars (secondary).
- **[S32]** *Relation between active mediation, exposure to Daniel Tiger's Neighborhood, and US preschoolers' social and emotional development.* Rasmussen, E. E., et al. *Journal of Children and Media* 10(4), 443–461. 2016. https://doi.org/10.1080/17482798.2016.1203806 — Experiment (n = 127, ages 2–6, 10 episodes over 2 weeks). © Taylor & Francis. Note: gains in empathy, self-efficacy and emotion recognition appeared **only when parents regularly talked about the content**. Effects were strongest for younger and lower-income children.
- **[S33]** *Media and Young Minds* (policy statement). American Academy of Pediatrics, Council on Communications and Media. *Pediatrics* 138(5), e20162591. 2016. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2016-2591 — Clinical guideline. © AAP. Note: for ages 2–5, limit screen use to about 1 hour a day of high-quality programming, co-view and apply it to the real world, and keep screens away from meals and the hour before bed.
- **[S34]** *Australian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines* (Early Years, birth–5, 2017; Children and Young People, 5–17, 2019). Australian Government Department of Health. https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians — Government guideline. © Commonwealth; licence not verified. Note: ages 3–5 should have no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day ("less is better"). Ages 5–17 should have no more than 2 hours of **recreational** screen time a day, and the guideline distinguishes recreational from educational use. 5–17-year-olds should get at least 60 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity. Ethan crosses from the 3–5 band into the 5–17 band.
- **[S35]** Khan Academy Kids (official site) and Common Sense Education review. Khan Academy; Common Sense Media. Undated. https://learn.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-kids/ ; https://www.commonsense.org/education/app/khan-academy-kids — Product page and review. © owners. Note: free and ad-free, for ages 2–8. Its curriculum was developed with Stanford GSE educators. Content rotates among maths, ELA, logic, executive function, SEL and creative expression. No independent outcome study was found.
- **[S36]** *Breathe, Think, Do with Sesame* (review). Common Sense Education. Undated. https://www.commonsense.org/education/app/breathe-think-do-with-sesame — App review. © Common Sense. Note: the app teaches a calm-down routine (belly breathing, then thinking of a plan, then doing it) through a monster's everyday problems. It describes itself as "research-based", but no peer-reviewed outcome study was found. The reviewer notes the breathing segments run long for young children.
- **[S37]** *What matters for young children's healthy digital media use and learning* (Rachel Barr, PhD). Children and Screens. Undated. https://www.childrenandscreens.org/learn-explore/research/what-matters-for-young-childrens-healthy-digital-media-use-and-learning-rachel-barr-phd/ — Expert summary (secondary). © publisher. Note: the "video deficit" means young children learn less from one-way screens than from live interaction. A responsive adult, or a contingent and responsive medium, reduces the gap. The evidence is mostly for children under 3.
